Q4 [clear] What is number matching in multi-factor authentication?
   MODE: basic | search: What is number matching in multi-factor authentication? | hit=True
ANSWER: According to the context, number matching in multi-factor authentication (MFA) is a method that can mitigate MFA fatigue. It involves requiring the same phone number to be used for both MFA and the primary phone number associated with the account. This way, even if a malicious actor sends a multitude of approve or deny "push requests" to a user's phone, the user will not be able to authenticate with the compromised user's credentials, as the phone number used for MFA must match the primary phone number.

Q4 [clear] What is number matching in multi-factor authentication?
   MODE: both | search: Number matching in multi-factor authentication | hit=True
ANSWER: According to [1] (CSI-PHISHING-GUIDANCE.pdf, p.4) and [4] (CSI-PHISHING-GUIDANCE.pdf, p.4), number matching in multi-factor authentication refers to the practice of requiring a specific phone number to be associated with an account in order to receive SMS or voice-based MFA codes. This is done to prevent malicious actors from convincing cellular carrier representatives to transfer control of a user's phone number to receive MFA codes, thereby allowing them to authenticate with the compromised user's credentials.

* Create a different 6-digit PIN/password for each device, and avoid using the same one.
* Avoid using guessable PINs like 123456 or 000000.

Q11 [vague] how do companies stop bad messages reaching staff?
   MODE: basic | search: how do companies stop bad messages reaching staff? | hit=False
ANSWER: Companies can stop bad messages reaching staff by implementing the following measures:

1. Enable Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) for received emails, which rejects any incoming email that has a domain that is being spoofed when a DMARC policy of reject is enabled.
2. Implement internal mail and messaging monitoring to identify suspicious activity and establish a baseline of normal network traffic.
3. Use free security tools, such as OpenDNS Home, to prevent cyber threat actors from redirecting users to malicious websites to steal their credentials.
4. Harden credentials by implementing user training on social engineering and phishing attacks, and by performing an account lockout when unusual activity or ongoing malicious login attempts are occurring.
5. Use denylists at the email gateway and enable firewall rules to prevent successful malware deployment.
6. Remove any unnecessary content from the organization's website and social media pages to reduce the likelihood of receiving scam messages.
7. Review and update privacy settings within social media accounts to prevent sharing of personal information.
8. Use separate business and personal accounts for social media, and treat personal social media like a business account.

These measures can help prevent bad messages from reaching staff and reduce the risk of phishing attacks.

Q12 [vague] how can I tell if a message is fake?
   MODE: basic | search: how can I tell if a message is fake? | hit=True
ANSWER: According to [4] (Small organisations guide to cyber security _ Spotting cyber attacks _ National Cyber Security Centre.pdf, p.2), you can identify a fake message by looking out for the following signs:

* Many phishing messages look convincing and may pretend to be from someone you know or trust.
* They may contain links to websites controlled by criminals, which may download a virus onto your computer, or steal your login/bank details.
* They may ask you to access sensitive business details, or your customers' details, or your email (from which they can gain control of all your other accounts).

It's also recommended to:

* If you have any doubts about a message, contact the organisation directly.
* Don't use the numbers or links in the message – use the details from their official website.
* If you think you've clicked on a suspicious message, or you've been tricked into sharing your password (or other personal information), don't panic. Read the NCSC's phishing advice on steps to take to protect yourself, and follow the appropriate steps.
* Consider what information is on your organisation's website and social media pages, and what detail is unnecessary (but could be useful for criminals).
* Review your privacy settings within your social media accounts, and use separate business and personal accounts for social media.
